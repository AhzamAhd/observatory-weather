% ============ ABSTRACT ============
\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.9\textwidth}
{\small
\textbf{Abstract.} GOWC (Global Observing-conditions Weather Catalogue) is an open web
application that takes the user from a target to an exposure plan across $2{,}642$
catalogued observatory sites, computing seeing, airmass, extinction, precipitable
water vapour, sky brightness and signal-to-noise from live Open-Meteo meteorology
including pressure-level profiles, and testing each output against an independent
reference. The photometric SNR calculator, run with GOWC's own catalogue entries,
reproduces the ING \textsc{signal} exposure-time calculator to a median $1.9\%$ over a
16-point magnitude--airmass grid on two instruments. Precipitable water vapour tracks
the Paranal LHATPRO radiometer closely ($\rho=+0.95$, 606 nights) with a $+43\%$ wet
bias. Seeing is computed from the Tatarski $C_n^2$ chain integrated over the pressure
levels above the site and calibrated to the ESO MASS free-atmosphere median at
Paranal; matching it requires an effective Tatarski coefficient of $5.7$, about twice
the published range, which quantifies how much turbulence four coarse pressure levels
fail to resolve. Against $\sim$20 months of ESO DIMM data the seeing forecast has a
weak but significant night-to-night ranking skill ($\rho=+0.24$, 575 nights, at
Paranal; $+0.16$ at La Silla). Surface wind alone does as well ($+0.27$, $+0.41$) and
persistence does better ($+0.42$, $+0.47$), but the free-atmosphere term tracks MASS
($\rho=+0.35$) and carries information that surface wind does not (partial
$\rho=+0.28$, $+0.40$). The absolute seeing scale at held-out sites is no better than
a constant. GOWC therefore treats forecast seeing as indicative, displays the measured
value wherever a public site monitor exists, and states quantity by quantity which
outputs are measurement-backed. It is a planning aid, not a substitute for on-site
measurement.

% ============ 1. INTRODUCTION ============
\section{Introduction}

Planning a night at an optical telescope involves several steps. The observer
first confirms that the target reaches a usable altitude during astronomical
darkness, then estimates the conditions for that night (seeing, transparency, and
sky brightness with the Moon up), and finally sets an exposure time for the
required precision. Each step depends on the one before it, but the tools do not:
visibility comes from planetarium software, conditions from a weather page or
forecast, and signal-to-noise from an instrument-specific exposure-time
calculator (ETC).

GOWC (Global Observing-conditions Weather Catalogue) combines this workflow in one open web
application covering $2{,}642$ catalogued observatory sites. Its design
priority is that every reported number can be traced to a published physical
model with stated assumptions. This paper describes those models, tests them against
site monitors and a professional ETC, and states where the method breaks down. The
results support exposure-time planning, airmass and dark-time scheduling, and a
water-vapour forecast with a known bias; forecast seeing is indicative only.

This paper makes four contributions. First, it sets out a complete, reproducible
forward planning chain --- visibility, seeing, extinction, precipitable water vapour,
sky brightness and photometric SNR --- driven by one shared physics module across a
catalogue of $2{,}642$ sites rather than a single facility. Second, it benchmarks the
SNR path, with the instrument constants an observer actually gets, against a
professional exposure-time calculator across a magnitude--airmass grid. Third, it
measures the seeing forecast's night-to-night skill over a 20-month hindcast against
the ESO Astronomical Site Monitor, against the baselines an observer already has ---
persistence and surface wind --- and reports what the calibration implies about the
input data. Fourth, it states quantity by quantity which outputs are
measurement-backed, which are calibrated, and which are passed through unvalidated.

% ============ 2. SYSTEM OVERVIEW ============
\section{System Overview}

GOWC is a full-stack application. The observatory list is built from the IAU
Minor Planet Center observatory-code list (retrieved 2026-08-31, names and
coordinates re-derived from the list of 2026-10-16; with several flagship research
facilities added by hand), giving $2{,}642$ catalogued sites. The MPC codes include
many small and amateur stations, so this is a count of catalogued observatory sites
rather than of major professional facilities. Each site's geodetic latitude is
recovered from the MPC parallax constants $(\rho\cos\phi',\rho\sin\phi')$ by the
standard iterative geocentric-to-geodetic conversion on the WGS84 ellipsoid, and its
elevation is taken from a digital-elevation lookup at those coordinates. (The
geocentric latitude $\phi'=\operatorname{atan2}(\rho\sin\phi',\rho\cos\phi')$ lies up
to $0.19^\circ$, $\sim$21\,km, equatorward of the true site, enough to move an
elevation lookup by hundreds of metres in mountainous terrain, so the conversion
matters.) About $1.4\%$ of MPC entries carry
only coarse coordinates (longitude to $\le$0.01$^\circ$ or parallax constants to
$\le$3 decimals); for those the elevation --- and hence the altitude-dependent seeing
and extinction --- remains uncertain.

A scheduled job (GitHub Actions, twice daily at 05 and 17\,UTC) fetches weather for
every observatory from Open-Meteo, one request per distinct location shared by
co-located sites: surface fields (cloud, humidity, wind, temperature, dew point,
pressure, precipitation), pressure-level profiles --- temperature, geopotential height
and wind at 850, 700, 600 and 500\,hPa, wind at 250\,hPa (the jet stream), and
relative humidity at 850 and 500\,hPa --- and the model total-column water vapour. All
winds are requested in m\,s$^{-1}$. Each fetch stores the fields for the forecast hour
nearest the fetch time, so the application reports conditions at that hour rather
than a night-integrated forecast; at 17\,UTC that hour is daytime in Chile. A shared
physics module converts the stored fields into observing quantities, served by two
front-ends --- an interactive dashboard and a REST API --- which pass the full
pressure-level profile to the same functions. A guided planning workflow runs on top
of the physics engine: it handles target and site selection, computes visibility and
the best window, applies the conditions, solves for exposure and SNR, and produces an
exportable observing plan.

\paragraph{Scope: the forward half of the pipeline.} The standard photometric
workflow has two halves. The \emph{forward} (predictive) half turns a target
magnitude into expected detector counts and a signal-to-noise ratio, through the
chain flux $\to$ magnitude $\to$ atmospheric extinction $\to$ collected photons
$\to$ SNR. The \emph{reverse} (reduction) half recovers a calibrated magnitude
from a real frame: centroiding and PSF fitting, aperture and annulus sky
estimation, relative photometry against comparison stars, and zero-point
calibration against standard stars. GOWC implements the forward half in full ---
including the Vega/UBVRI system, the Bouguer extinction law (with the secondary,
colour-dependent term $k = k_1 + k_2(B\!-\!V)$ reported alongside the result), and the
instrumental-to-standard colour term --- and predicts the magnitude uncertainty
$\sigma_m = 1.0857/\mathrm{SNR}$ that the reverse half would ultimately measure.
It does not perform the reduction steps, because it never handles a real image.

\paragraph{Provided tools.} Beyond the core physics, GOWC exposes a set of
observer-facing tools, all driven by the same engine and live data. For
planning: a live weather map of all sites, object-visibility and observing-window
calculators, site comparison, a guided ``Plan an Observation'' wizard, and the
SNR/exposure calculator. For analysis: atmospheric analysis (seeing, extinction,
jet stream, PWV), historical reliability, and telescope efficiency. For transient
and event work: a Transient Follow-Up tab that lists active neutron-star X-ray
binaries with live outburst alerts from the MAXI monitor (this first version
covers X-ray binaries; other classes are flagged as forthcoming), and a Sky
Events section with trackers for meteor showers, eclipses, comets, satellite
passes, and near-Earth asteroids (the latter drawn live from NASA's NeoWs/NEO
service). A literature-search tool queries the NASA ADS database. An observing
assistant answers plain-language planning questions: a large language model
(Anthropic's Claude, through its API) only parses the question into structured
parameters and phrases the answer, while every number in the answer comes from the
deterministic engine; the assistant is rate-limited and declines when the engine
cannot answer. Registered users can save sites and log sessions.

\paragraph{Relation to existing tools.} The individual computations GOWC performs
are available elsewhere, but in separate tools: target visibility and airmass in
Astroplan \cite{astroplan}; sky brightness, extinction and exposure times in the
ESO exposure-time calculators and \textsc{skycalc} \cite{skycalc}; instrument-specific
SNR in per-facility calculators such as ING \textsc{signal} \cite{signal}. What GOWC
adds is their \emph{combination} in one place --- visibility, live atmospheric
conditions and photometric SNR, computed from one shared physics module --- across a
global catalogue of $\sim$2{,}600 sites rather than a single facility, with each
output traceable to a published model and, where possible, tested against a site
monitor. It is the breadth (many sites, the whole planning chain) rather than the
depth at any one facility that distinguishes it.

\paragraph{Installation, usage and reproducibility.} GOWC runs as a hosted web
application (\texttt{gowcastroclimate.com}); the physics engine is a standalone
Python package (Python $\ge$3.10; dependencies \texttt{numpy}, \texttt{pandas},
\texttt{astropy}, \texttt{ephem}, \texttt{requests}) installable from the repository
with \texttt{pip install .}\ and usable without the web layer. The core entry points
are direct function calls --- for example
\begin{quote}\small\texttt{rank\_sites(ra\_deg, dec\_deg, date\_utc)}\end{quote}
returns the candidate observatories ranked by tonight's observability (airmass,
dark-time window and moonlit sky) for a target, and
\texttt{get\_full\_atmospheric\_analysis(record)} returns the seeing, PWV and
jet-stream diagnostics for a site from its weather record. Every number in
Tables~1--6 and Figures~2--4 (Figure~1 is a schematic) is produced by a re-runnable
script in \texttt{tests/}: \texttt{validate\_\allowbreak snr\_\allowbreak etc.py} and
\texttt{snr\_\allowbreak grid\_\allowbreak compare.py} (Tables~1 and 6, Appendix~B),
\texttt{dimm\_\allowbreak mass\_\allowbreak decomposition.py} (Table~2),
\texttt{site\_\allowbreak bias.py} (Table~3),
\texttt{hindcast\_\allowbreak multiyear.py} (calibration constants, Table~4,
baselines) and \texttt{hindcast\_\allowbreak extras.py} (per-year and
out-of-window splits, partial correlations, poor-night skill),
\texttt{validate\_\allowbreak pwv\_\allowbreak lhatpro.py} (\S4.2),
\texttt{validate\_airmass.py} and \texttt{validate\_extinction.py} (\S4.1), and
\texttt{make\_\allowbreak paper\_\allowbreak figures.py} /
\texttt{make\_\allowbreak arch\_\allowbreak figure.py} (figures). Wiring these into the
Actions workflow as a regression check is a planned step. This paper describes GOWC
v1.0.1 (MIT-licensed); the exact version is archived at the Zenodo DOI given in the Data
and Code Availability section.

% ============ 3. METHODS ============
\section{Physical Models}

All quantities below are computed from the cited formalism. Constants are quoted
as implemented.

\subsection{Seeing}
\label{sec:seeing}
Seeing (FWHM, arcsec) is derived from the Tatarski $C_n^2$ formulation
\cite{tatarski} driven by real vertical temperature gradients from the pressure
levels above the site (850/700/600/500\,hPa), following the modern re-derivation of
Basu \& Holtslag \cite{basu}. With potential temperature $\theta = T(1000/P)^{R/c_p}$:
\[
\Gamma = \frac{d\theta}{dz},\quad
C_T^2 = \frac{c}{\mathrm{Pr}_t}L_0^{4/3}\Big(\tfrac{T}{\theta}\Big)^{2}\Gamma^2,\quad
C_n^2 = \Big(\tfrac{79\times10^{-6}P}{T^2}\Big)^{2}C_T^2,
\]
\[
r_0 = \big(0.423\,k^2 X\!\int\! C_n^2\,dh\big)^{-3/5},\qquad
\varepsilon = 0.98\,\lambda/r_0,
\]
with $k=2\pi/\lambda$, the Fried parameter $r_0$ \cite{fried} referenced at
$\lambda=500$\,nm, $\mathrm{Pr}_t=1$ the turbulent Prandtl number for stable
night-time stratification, and seeing FWHM written as $\varepsilon$. The remaining
choices are stated explicitly:

\emph{Airmass.} $X$ appears \emph{inside} the $C_n^2$ integral bracket (slant-path
convention), i.e.\ the turbulence integral is scaled by $X$ before the $-3/5$ power.

\emph{Potential- to actual-temperature conversion.} The $d\theta/dz$ mixing-length
argument yields $C_\theta^2$, the structure constant of \emph{potential}
temperature, but the optical conversion acts on \emph{actual}-temperature
fluctuations, $T' = (T/\theta)\theta'$; hence $C_T^2 = (T/\theta)^2 C_\theta^2$
\cite{basu}. The factor is altitude-dependent ($(T/\theta)^2 = 0.91$ at 850\,hPa,
$0.67$ at 500\,hPa, $\approx0.78$ mid-layer, a $\sim$14\% effect on seeing); it is
evaluated with each layer's mean $T$ and $\theta$.

\emph{Outer scale.} The outer scale enters $C_T^2$ as $L_0^{4/3}$, taken from the
Dewan et al.\ (1993) tropospheric model \cite{dewan}, the standard in NWP-derived
optical-turbulence work \cite{trinquet}:
\[
L_0^{4/3} = 0.1^{4/3}\times 10^{\,1.64 + 42\,S},
\]
with wind shear $S$ in s$^{-1}$ from winds in m\,s$^{-1}$. Dewan's fit takes the
\emph{local} shear, so $S$ is the shear across each individual layer, matching the
layer over which its $\Gamma$ is formed. Because $S$ sits in an exponent,
$\partial(\ln\varepsilon)/\partial S \approx 58$\,s: a shear error of
$0.001$\,s$^{-1}$ is a $\sim$6\% seeing error. That sensitivity is why the
winds must be in m\,s$^{-1}$: at Paranal the per-layer shear has a median of
$2.2\times10^{-3}$\,s$^{-1}$ (95th percentile $6.7\times10^{-3}$), whereas the same
winds read as km\,h$^{-1}$ triple it. Two guards are applied: $S$ is capped at
$0.02$\,s$^{-1}$, the strong-shear end of Dewan's fit (reached in 0.01\% of Paranal
layers), and a representative $3\times10^{-3}$\,s$^{-1}$ is used where no shear is
available. A difference across a model layer still under-resolves the thin-layer
shear the Dewan radiosonde fit was built on.

\emph{Free-atmosphere integration and calibration.} GOWC integrates $C_n^2$ over the
pressure levels that lie \emph{above} the site --- 850, 700, 600 and 500\,hPa;
250\,hPa supplies only the jet-stream wind --- forming $\Gamma$, the Dewan $L_0$ and
$C_n^2$ for each adjacent layer and weighting by that layer's geometric depth, with
the lowest bracket interpolated to the telescope elevation. Levels below the site
are excluded, removing the sub-site slab and the Atacama marine inversion near
1000--1500\,m. For a site below 850\,hPa ($\sim$1500\,m) there is no bracket to
interpolate, so the integral starts at 850\,hPa and the slab beneath is left to the
ground-layer term. A single dimensionless constant $C$ scales the integral. It is
calibrated at Paranal so that the model's free-atmosphere median over the 915 hours
matched to ESO MASS measurements (2026-05-29 to 08-29) equals the MASS median over
the same hours ($0.45''$), giving $C=2.03$. The Tatarski coefficient $c$ multiplies
the same integral, so the two are degenerate and only $c_{\text{eff}}=c\,C$ is
identifiable: with $c=2.8$, $c_{\text{eff}}=5.7$. That is about twice the
$2.1$--$3.2$ quoted in the literature. We read it as a measurement, not a fit to be
defended: four pressure levels resolve only the broad gradients, not the thin, sharp
sheets where optical turbulence concentrates, and the calibration absorbs a factor
of about two in $\int C_n^2\,dh$ that the coarse profile misses. The same constant serves the live Forecast API
and the Historical Forecast API used for validation: on 48 Paranal readings stored by
the live site since 2026-08-25, the two products give free-atmosphere medians of
$0.50''$ and $0.49''$ with a rank correlation of $0.99$. If the 700/600\,hPa levels
are missing, a two-level fallback uses the 850$\to$500\,hPa gradient over an effective
layer thickness, calibrated the same way to $4900$\,m.

One definitional caveat attaches to the lower bound. MASS measures turbulence above
$\sim$500\,m above the site, whereas the integral begins at the telescope, so at
Paranal the slab from $2635$\,m to the 700\,hPa level ($\sim$3150\,m) sits inside
$\varepsilon_{\text{free}}$ while the DIMM$-$MASS difference of \S\ref{sec:dimm}
assigns it to the ground layer. The two terms therefore overlap by roughly one layer;
the calibration constants absorb this numerically.

\emph{Ground layer.} The pressure levels resolve only the free atmosphere, so the
free-atmosphere value is combined in quadrature with a ground/boundary-layer term
(independent turbulent layers),
$\varepsilon_{\text{tot}} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{free}}^2 +
\varepsilon_{\text{bl}}^2}$. The ground layer is a calibrated surface
parametrisation of our own:
\[
\varepsilon_{\text{bl}} = \varepsilon_0\,
\big(1 + (w/8)^2\big)^{0.3}\,
e^{-h/10000}\; X^{0.6},
\]
with surface wind $w$ (m\,s$^{-1}$), site altitude $h$ (m) and $\varepsilon_0 =
0.77''$, set so that Paranal at its median surface wind ($2.8$\,m\,s$^{-1}$)
reproduces the measured ground-layer median of $0.61''$ (\S\ref{sec:dimm}). It grows
with surface wind and depends only gently on altitude: the ground layer is set by
local topography and heating, and the MASS/DIMM decomposition confirms that even
Paranal retains a strong one. The $10\,000$\,m scale height is chosen, not fitted ---
one site cannot constrain it --- and makes the term nearly site-invariant (altitude
factor $1.00$ at sea level to $0.64$ at 4500\,m). A humidity factor was tested as a
surface-instability proxy and reduced ranking skill, so it is not included.

\emph{Fallback.} Where a site has no usable profile (and for the historical
reliability page, whose daily archive stores surface fields only), seeing is the same
ground-layer term combined in quadrature with the calibrated median free-atmosphere
seeing, $0.45''X^{0.6}$, and is indicative only.

\subsection{Airmass and extinction}
A single airmass definition is used throughout --- target visibility, extinction,
scintillation and the SNR path --- the Pickering (2002) interpolative formula
\cite{pickering}: $X = 1/\sin\!\big(a + 244/(165+47a^{1.1})\big)$, $a$ in degrees. The
one exception is the moonlit-sky model of \S3.4, which uses Krisciunas \& Schaefer's
own airmass expression as part of that published model. Extinction follows the
Bouguer law $\Delta m = k\,X$ with band coefficients anchored to La Palma monitoring
\cite{king} and scaled to site altitude by a single exponential,
$k(h) = k_{\text{ref}}\,e^{-(h - h_{\text{ref}})/H_k}$ with $H_k = 8000$\,m; the scale
factor is clamped to $0.70$--$1.8$, so sites above $\sim$4850\,m take the high-site
limit. Transmission is $10^{-0.4\,k X}$. A single scale height is a deliberate
simplification: the Rayleigh component scales with pressure ($H\approx8400$\,m) while
the aerosol component falls far faster ($H\approx1$--2\,km), and the aerosol term
(which dominates $k$ at low sites) is the larger source of residual error. When a
target colour is known the secondary term $k = k_1 + k_2(B\!-\!V)$ is reported
alongside the result but not applied to the single-band $k$
(Appendix~\ref{app:constants}); likewise the colour term
$m_{\text{std}} = m_{\text{inst}} + \text{zp} + c_t\,(B\!-\!V)$.

\subsection{Precipitable water vapour}
PWV uses the data provider's model-computed total-column water vapour --- a
radiative-transfer product, more reliable than integrating a coarse two-node humidity
profile. The provider integrates the column from the model terrain height
$h_{\text{model}}$ returned with each query, so only the residual water between that
surface and the telescope is removed: $\mathrm{PWV}(h_{\text{site}}) =
\mathrm{PWV}_{\text{model}}\, e^{-(h_{\text{site}}-h_{\text{model}})/H_w}$ with
$H_w \approx 2000$\,m. (Scaling from sea level instead, $e^{-h_{\text{site}}/H_w}$,
removes the sub-site column twice; the LHATPRO comparison in \S\ref{sec:pwv} caught
this.) A layered integral of specific humidity from the Magnus relation \cite{magnus}
is retained as a fallback where the column product is unavailable.

\subsection{Sky brightness}
\label{sec:sky}
Moonlit sky brightness uses the Krisciunas \& Schaefer (1991) model \cite{ks1991}
in the $V$ band. A zenith dark-sky baseline of $21.8$\,mag\,arcsec$^{-2}$ is carried
to the target's line of sight as $B_{\text{zen}}\,10^{-0.4k(X-1)}X$ (their Eq.~2),
and the scattered moonlight is added; it depends on the lunar phase angle $\alpha$,
the Moon's and target's airmasses, and strongly on the Moon--target separation.
The extinction coefficient $k$ in both terms is the site's own $V$-band value
(\S3.2). The phase angle follows from the illuminated fraction $f$ as
$\alpha=\arccos(2f-1)$; a linear mapping would be $\sim$0.4\,mag wrong in the gibbous
phases. The geometry --- target altitude, Moon altitude and illumination, and their
separation --- is computed with \texttt{ephem} for the site and time on every page
that uses the sky: the SNR calculator, the per-hour peak-time ranking, the all-site
SNR comparison (each site at its own target altitude) and the planning workflow,
which uses the real Moon at the planned time or, for a what-if Moon phase, places
the Moon an assumed $90^\circ$ from the target. The $V$-band value is used for every
filter, a simplification of the full model.

\subsection{Signal-to-noise ratio}
The SNR calculator implements the standard CCD equation with scintillation:
\[
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{S}{\sqrt{S + B_{\mathrm{sky}} + D_c + N_{\mathrm{read}}^2 n_{\mathrm{pix}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{scint}}^2}},
\]
where $S$ is source counts and $B_{\mathrm{sky}}$ and $D_c$ are the sky-background
and dark-current electron totals summed over the $n_{\mathrm{pix}}$ pixels of the
aperture (a 1-FWHM radius), and $N_{\mathrm{read}}$ is the per-pixel read noise
($D_c$ is distinct from the telescope diameter $D$ below and from DIMM seeing in
\S\ref{sec:dimm}). Source flux uses per-band Vega zero-points \cite{bessell}.
Scintillation follows Osborn et al.\ (2015) \cite{osborn},
$\sigma_Y^2 = 10^{-5}C_Y^2 D^{-4/3}t^{-1}X^3 e^{-2h/H}$ ($H=8000$\,m, $C_Y\approx1.5$
\cite{kornilov}); $\sigma_Y$ is a fractional fluctuation, so it enters the noise
budget as $\sigma_{\text{scint}} = \sigma_Y\,S$. The sky is attenuated by the same
extinction as the source. Strictly, a sky brightness measured from the ground already
includes the atmosphere, so this understates the sky by $10^{-0.4kX}$ ($\sim$11\% at
$X=1$); it is the \textsc{signal} convention, and the faint-end benchmark confirms it
(removing the attenuation moves the sky-limited ACAM points from $-0.2\%$ to $-5$ to
$-7\%$ against \textsc{signal}). The exposure time for a target SNR solves
$S^2t^2 - \mathrm{SNR}^2(S+B+D_c+KS^2)\,t - \mathrm{SNR}^2 R = 0$, where $S$, $B$ and
$D_c$ are now rates, $R$ is the total read-noise variance, and $K=\sigma_Y^2t$ is
constant because $\sigma_Y^2\propto t^{-1}$, so scintillation, like shot noise, enters
linearly in $t$ and the solution is exact (e.g.\ INT/WFC at $V=20$ reaches SNR\,100 in
119\,s; a $V=8$ star reaches SNR\,30 in 5\,ms, well below a practical shutter time,
which the planner flags together with saturation). Instrument constants (aperture,
obstruction, pixel scale, read noise, full well, throughput, QE, and an
empirical/theoretical efficiency) come from a small cited catalogue
(Appendix~\ref{app:constants}). The magnitude uncertainty is reported as
$\sigma_m = 1.0857/\mathrm{SNR}$.

% ============ 4. VALIDATION ============
\section{Validation}

Each quantity is tested against the best available reference: deterministic
quantities against reference tables, and atmospheric quantities against instrument
measurements where a public site monitor exists.

\subsection{Airmass and extinction}
Airmass is deterministic geometry, checked against the Kasten \& Young (1989)
reference tables \cite{kasten}: GOWC's Pickering values agree to better than
$0.35\%$ above $5^\circ$ altitude (largest deviation $0.34\%$, at $10^\circ$), and the
two forms diverge to $\sim$1.6\% at $0.1^\circ$, far below any observing limit.
Extinction coefficients are \emph{anchored} to King (1985) at La Palma \cite{king}
(ORM, taken as 2360\,m), so La Palma agreement is by construction; the
altitude-scaled coefficients match the published Paranal values to
$\le$0.01\,mag/airmass in $BVRI$ ($0.006$ in $V$) and $0.02$ in $U$. This is a weak
test of the altitude scaling: the 275\,m step from La Palma to Paranal changes the
coefficient by only 3.4\%, so the agreement chiefly re-tests the anchor, and the
extinction scale height itself is essentially unconstrained here.

\subsection{Precipitable water vapour against LHATPRO}
\label{sec:pwv}
GOWC's model column, corrected to the site (\S3.3), is compared with the ESO LHATPRO
radiometer at Paranal over 2025-01 to 2026-08, as nightly medians with nights keyed
noon to noon and the model sampled only at the hours LHATPRO measured ($N=606$). The
night-to-night agreement is excellent: Spearman $\rho=+0.945$ ($[+0.94,+0.95]$),
Pearson $r=+0.96$. The comparison exposed a real bug: Open-Meteo integrates the
column from the model's terrain surface ($2617$\,m for the Paranal cell), not from
sea level, so scaling by $e^{-h_{\text{site}}/H_w}$ removed the sub-site water a
second time and biased the column low by a factor $3.7$ ($2.6\times$ below the
measurement) --- worse with altitude, across the whole catalogue. With the correction
the model median is $3.9$\,mm against a measured $2.7$\,mm, a $+43\%$ wet bias
(median per-night ratio $1.34$). The bias is only mildly larger on the driest nights
that matter most for the infrared: $1.44\times$ for nights below 1\,mm (28 nights)
against $1.35\times$ above 4\,mm. GOWC therefore reports PWV as a validated quantity
for ranking ($\rho=0.95$) with a stated absolute bias, and displays the LHATPRO
measurement where it exists (Figure~\ref{fig:pwv}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig_pwv.png}
\caption{GOWC model-column PWV (corrected to the model terrain, \S3.3) against the

Two caveats bound this test. At Paranal the model cell ($h_{\text{model}}=2617$\,m)
sits essentially at the site ($2635$\,m), so the residual correction is
$e^{-18/2000}=0.99$: LHATPRO validates the \emph{raw} Open-Meteo column and diagnoses
the double-count, but says nothing about the exponential form or $H_w=2000$\,m,
which remain unconstrained here. Testing them needs a site with a large
$h_{\text{site}}-h_{\text{model}}$ gap and a public PWV feed. The $+43\%$ wet bias is
a property of the forecast column, which we do not claim to explain.

\subsection{Cloud cover: passed through, not validated}
Cloud cover is the most decisive input for whether a night happens at all; GOWC uses
the provider's forecast in the headline observing-quality score without independent
validation. A satellite-cloud or all-sky-camera comparison is the obvious next test;
the quantity is flagged as unvalidated wherever it drives a reported score.

which enters the calibration.

\paragraph{Night-to-night ranking over 20 months.} Bias and ranking are independent,
so ranking is tested directly. GOWC's seeing is hindcast through Open-Meteo's
Historical Forecast API, which archives pressure-level forecast fields (unlike the
ERA5 archive endpoint, which serves no pressure levels, or the live API, which keeps
$\sim$12 days), with every wind converted to m\,s$^{-1}$, and time-matched to the ESO
ASM DIMM record over 2025-01-01 to 2026-08-28. The ASM DIMM reports seeing corrected
to zenith, so the model is evaluated at $X=1$. DIMM seeing autocorrelates over 1--3
hours, so each observing night --- keyed noon to noon in local time --- is reduced to
one median, and the model is sampled only at the hours the DIMM measured, so its
nightly value never includes daytime. This archive stores each run's initial hours,
i.e.\ near-analysis rather than a multi-day-ahead forecast, so every $\rho$ below is an
\emph{upper bound} on operational planning skill. Results are in

Five points summarise the hindcast. (i) Every interval at Paranal excludes zero, so
the model does carry night-to-night information, but it is weak:
$\rho^2\approx6\%$ of the rank variance, a debiased log scatter of $0.15$\,dex, and a
strongly compressed dynamic range. (ii) It is not a seasonal artefact (deseasonalised
$\rho=+0.26$ at Paranal, $+0.36$ at La Silla) and not an artefact of the calibration
window ($+0.25$ outside it). (iii) The baselines an observer already has do as well or
better. Surface wind alone scores $+0.27$ at Paranal and $+0.41$ at La Silla, and
persistence $+0.42$ and $+0.47$; the full model beats neither. Controlling for
persistence, the model retains a partial $\rho$ of $+0.22$ (Paranal) and $+0.18$ (La
Silla). (iv) The free-atmosphere term is the part that carries independent
information. Its marginal skill against DIMM is modest ($+0.21$, $+0.14$), but it
tracks MASS ($\rho=+0.35$, a test immune to the calibration, since $C$ is a
monotonic scale on $\varepsilon_{\text{free}}$ and $\varepsilon_0$ does not enter it),
and controlling for surface wind it retains a partial $\rho$ of $+0.28$ at Paranal and
$+0.40$ at La Silla. (v) The weakness is therefore in the combination, not only in
the inputs. Surface wind and the free-atmosphere term each carry information, but
adding them in quadrature with weights calibrated to Paranal's median conditions does
not exploit it; at La Silla the combined forecast scores below its own ground-layer
input ($+0.16$ against $+0.41$). The ground-layer term is, at a fixed site, just
surface wind re-expressed, so its skill equals the wind's.

Cast as an operational question, ``will tonight be poor?'' (DIMM $>1.0''$, a 21\%
base rate at Paranal), a rate-matched model threshold flags such nights with a 36\%
hit rate at a 19\% false-alarm rate; when it flags a night as poor it is right 33\% of
the time, a $1.6\times$ enrichment over the base rate --- measured at a monitored site,
where the measured seeing is shown instead.

The magnitude follows from the input resolution. Dedicated mesoscale
optical-turbulence forecasts that resolve the vertical turbulence profile (e.g.\ the
MOSE/Astro-Meso-NH system at Paranal \cite{masciadri}) achieve much higher skill, at
far greater computational cost and for one site at a time. GOWC drives the published
Tatarski chain from four standard pressure levels across a global catalogue; the
$c_{\text{eff}}\approx5.7$ calibration and the compressed dynamic range both say that
those levels miss most of the structure that sets the seeing.

What is \emph{not} affected by this result is ranking sites for a given target ---
which observatory can observe an object tonight, for how long, at what airmass and
against what moonlit sky. That rests on airmass and dark-time geometry, validated to
sub-percent (\S4.1), with the anchored extinction of \S3.2 and the lunar sky model of
\S3.4; forecast seeing enters only as a secondary weight, and is replaced by a measured
DIMM value where one is published. Where a public site monitor exists GOWC
\emph{displays the measured value directly}: live DIMM seeing from the ESO Paranal ASM
and the Maunakea Weather Center, and live PWV from the Paranal LHATPRO radiometer.

\subsection{Sky brightness: modelled, not validated here}
\label{sec:skyval}
Moonlit sky brightness (\S3.4) is computed from the Krisciunas \& Schaefer (1991)
model \cite{ks1991}, which remains the standard photometric choice and is well tested
in its original literature. We do not independently validate it here; it sets the
sky term in every SNR and exposure estimate (\S\ref{sec:sky}), so its accuracy limits
those under a bright Moon.
A grid comparison against the ESO \textsc{skycalc} model \cite{skycalc} across lunar
phase and Moon--target separation is the natural next check (\S\ref{sec:future}); the
quantity is flagged as modelled-not-measured wherever it is reported.

% ============ 5. DISCUSSION ============
\section{Discussion and Limitations}

Every model in GOWC is taken from the published literature except one bespoke piece,
the ground-layer term $\varepsilon_{\text{bl}}$ (\S\ref{sec:seeing}), which is a
calibrated parametrisation of our own. The outputs separate into three groups.

\emph{On solid ground:} airmass ($<0.35\%$ against Kasten--Young above $5^\circ$),
the CCD SNR equation with GOWC's catalogued instruments (median $1.9\%$ against
\textsc{signal} over 16 points, Appendix~\ref{app:snrgrid}), the Bouguer extinction
form (anchored at La Palma; the Paranal check mainly re-tests the anchor), and
precipitable water vapour, which tracks LHATPRO night to night ($\rho=0.95$) with a
stated $+43\%$ wet bias.

\emph{Indicative:} forecast seeing. Its free-atmosphere term is physically
meaningful --- it tracks MASS and carries information independent of surface wind ---
but the full forecast ranks nights only weakly ($\rho=+0.24$ at Paranal, $+0.16$ at
La Silla), does no better than surface wind or persistence, and has no site-to-site
skill in absolute scale. The calibration requires $c_{\text{eff}}\approx5.7$, about
twice the literature range, which we interpret as the coarse vertical resolution
missing roughly half of $\int C_n^2\,dh$. Three things would improve it, in order of
cost: weighting the free-atmosphere and ground terms by a fit to the ranking rather
than to Paranal's medians (the partial correlations show both carry information that
the quadrature sum wastes); a third hindcast site in a different regime (e.g.\ the ING
RoboDIMM archive at La Palma) to test transferability, which is currently assumed,
not demonstrated; and higher vertical resolution.

\emph{Passed through or modelled only:} cloud cover (\S4.3) and moonlit sky
brightness (\S\ref{sec:skyval}). Aerosol and dust extinction are not modelled, and
some catalogued detectors use literature constants.

\paragraph{Forecast data and vertical resolution.}
\label{sec:dimm-inv}
GOWC uses \emph{forecast} fields, not on-site measurements; the forecast sets a floor
on accuracy that no downstream model can improve, and real-time transparency (thin
cirrus, Saharan dust) is not captured. Each stored record is the forecast hour nearest
the fetch, not a night-integrated value. Even with four pressure levels the profile
cannot resolve the thin turbulent sheets that dominate free-atmosphere seeing. At a
high site the 850\,hPa level ($\sim$1500\,m) lies below the telescope and, in the
Atacama, inside the marine subsidence inversion; integrating only above the site
removes that slab. A corollary is that the number of usable layers falls with site
altitude, because the integral stops at 500\,hPa ($\sim$5850\,m at Paranal): Paranal
has three levels above it; a site above the 600\,hPa level ($\sim$4400\,m) keeps only
500\,hPa and integrates a single layer; a site above 500\,hPa would fall back to the
two-level form (no catalogued site does; the highest is $5369$\,m). Fetching 300 and
250\,hPa temperatures would add layers at every site and is a straightforward
improvement.

\paragraph{Sky-brightness model.} The moonlight model (\S3.4) needs only quantities
GOWC already has, which is why it is used across the catalogue. More recent models are
more accurate: Jones et al.\ (2013) \cite{jones2013} replace the empirical fit with a
physical scattering calculation, and Winkler (2022) \cite{winkler2022} corrects a
lunar-beam path term. Both require site-specific aerosol characterisation and full
sky-background components that a forecast-driven planner covering thousands of sites
cannot supply.

\paragraph{Noise sources not modelled.} The SNR uses the standard CCD noise terms ---
source shot noise, sky, dark current, read noise, and scintillation --- which dominate
optical point-source photometry. Thermal background (significant only beyond
$\sim$1\,$\mu$m), flat-fielding residuals (a $\sim$0.1--1\% floor for very bright
sources), fringing, cosmic rays and charge-transfer inefficiency are omitted.

% ============ FUTURE WORK ============
\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

GOWC implements the forward half of the photometric pipeline. The natural next step
is to extend it toward the reverse half, reusing the calibration mathematics already
in the engine:

\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Image ingest.} Read calibrated FITS frames (via \texttt{astropy}).
\item \textbf{Instrumental calibration.} Bias, dark and flat-field correction with
cosmic-ray rejection.
\item \textbf{Aperture photometry.} Source detection, centroiding, annulus sky
estimation and aperture extraction (via \texttt{photutils}).
\item \textbf{Photometric calibration.} Feed those counts into the existing
extinction, zero-point and colour-term machinery.
\item \textbf{Science products.} Differential photometry and light curves.
\end{enumerate}

Four model refinements follow from \S4--5:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \textbf{Seeing combination.} Fit the relative weights of the free-atmosphere
and ground terms to the night-to-night ranking (with cross-validation across years),
since both carry independent information that the present quadrature sum does not use.
\item \textbf{More levels and more sites.} Add 300/250\,hPa temperatures, hindcast a
third site in a different regime, and measure skill against forecast lead time.
\item \textbf{Reanalysis cross-check.} Repeat the hindcast on ERA5 via the Copernicus
CDS, which is homogeneous where the Historical Forecast archive is not.
\item \textbf{Sky-brightness validation.} A grid comparison against \textsc{skycalc}
across lunar phase and Moon--target separation.
\end{itemize}

% ============ 6. CONCLUSION ============
\section{Conclusion}

GOWC combines visibility, atmospheric conditions and instrument performance in a
single open planning tool covering $2{,}642$ catalogued observatory sites. Its SNR
calculator, with GOWC's own instrument entries, agrees with the ING \textsc{signal}
ETC to a median $1.9\%$ across a magnitude--airmass grid; its precipitable water
vapour tracks the Paranal LHATPRO radiometer ($\rho=+0.95$) with a $+43\%$ wet bias.
Its seeing forecast, driven by four pressure levels, needs an effective Tatarski
coefficient twice the literature value and ranks nights only weakly ($\rho=+0.24$ at
Paranal over 20 months) --- no better than surface wind or persistence --- although
its free-atmosphere term tracks MASS and carries information the surface wind does
not. GOWC therefore treats forecast seeing as indicative, shows measured values
wherever a site monitor publishes them, and states for each output whether it is
measured, calibrated or passed through.

$h_{\text{ref}}=2000$\,m and scaled by $k(h)=k_{\text{ref}}\,e^{-(h-h_{\text{ref}})/H_k}$,
$H_k=8000$\,m, with the scale factor clamped to $0.70$--$1.8$. The values reproduce
the King (1985) ORM monitoring at 2360\,m (e.g.\ $k_V=0.125$ at $2000$\,m gives
$0.120$ at La Palma and $0.116$ at Paranal). The reference set is $k_U=0.479$,
$k_B=0.229$, $k_V=0.125$, $k_R=0.094$, $k_I=0.052$, and $k_J=0.031$, $k_H=0.021$,
$k_K=0.015$ (mag per airmass; the near-IR values are for transmission only). The
colour-dependent term $k=k_1+k_2(B\!-\!V)$ is reported but not applied to the
single-band $k$.

\section{SNR agreement across the observing regime}
\label{app:snrgrid}
Table~\ref{tab:snrgrid} compares GOWC, with its catalogue entries, against ING
\textsc{signal} on a grid in source magnitude ($m_V=16$--$22$, a factor $\sim$250 in
flux) and airmass ($X=1.0,1.5$; GOWC is evaluated at the altitude whose Pickering
airmass is exactly $X$), at 100\,s, $V$ band, dark sky ($21.50$), extinction $0.12$ and
$1.0''$ seeing. \textsc{signal} was run by hand; GOWC is recomputed by
\texttt{tests/snr\_grid\_compare.py}. Over the 16 points the median $|\Delta|$ is
$1.9\%$, the worst is $4.8\%$, and the median signed ratio is $0.999$.

The residuals are the sum of two effects, separated by re-running GOWC with its
scintillation term switched off (also in the script).

\emph{A constant throughput offset (INT/WFC).} With scintillation off, the WFC
residual is flat at $+3.0$ to $+3.6\%$ across all magnitudes and both airmasses.
GOWC's source and sky counts are both $\sim$6\% above \textsc{signal}'s, and a common
factor $(1+\delta)$ on source and sky scales the SNR by $\sqrt{1+\delta}$ in every
noise regime --- shot-noise- or sky-limited alike --- so a 6\% count offset appears as
a $\sim$3\% SNR offset everywhere. ACAM, with scintillation off, is flat at $-0.1$ to
$-0.4\%$.

\emph{Scintillation (both instruments).} The remaining structure grows with source
brightness and with airmass, the signature of the one noise term that scales with
the source counts and as $X^{1.5}$ in amplitude: at $X=1.5$ the ACAM residual runs
$-0.1,-0.3,-1.0,-4.8\%$ from $m_V=22$ to 16. For ACAM at $m_V=16$, GOWC's SNR falls
$5.7\%$ from $X=1.0$ to $1.5$ ($1256.7\to1185.0$) while \textsc{signal}'s falls $2.7\%$
($1280.0\to1245.1$); with scintillation off GOWC also falls $2.7\%$. The largest
disagreement in the grid is therefore \textsc{signal} omitting a term GOWC includes.
The same excess explains the one small WFC point, $m_V=16$, $X=1.5$ ($-0.4\%$), where
scintillation cancels the $+3\%$ throughput offset, and the smaller WFC offset at the
bright end at $X=1.0$ ($+1.9\%$ against $+3.0\%$ without scintillation).
